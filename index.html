Most account takeovers start with a password that was reused, guessed, or leaked in someone else's breach.

  • One password per account. A breach on one site must not unlock the rest.
  • Length beats complexity. Four random words beat P@ssw0rd!.
  • Use a password manager to generate and remember them for you.
  • Never type a password after tapping a link in an SMS or email.
  • Check for leaks at haveibeenpwned.com and change anything exposed.
If you can remember it without a manager, it is probably reused somewhere.
5 min read

A second lock turns a stolen password into a dead end — but only if the second factor is the right kind.

  • Turn on 2FA everywhere, starting with email and banking.
  • Prefer passkeys or an authenticator app over SMS codes.
  • Store backup codes offline — printed or in a manager, not a screenshot in chat.
  • Never approve a login you did not start. Repeated prompts mean someone has your password.
  • Codes arriving unrequested are a warning: change that password now.
Your email is the master key. Secure it first — it can reset everything else.
4 min read

A phishing message is a con with a deadline. It borrows a brand you trust and rushes you into a tap.

  • Urgency and fear are the tell. “Blocked”, “suspended”, “within 24 hours”.
  • Check the sender address, not the display name — both can be faked.
  • Do not tap. Open the official app or type the address yourself.
  • Look for lookalike domains: paypa1.com, arnazon.in, bank-secure.xyz.
  • Real companies never ask for your OTP, PIN, password or a “small fee” by link.
  • Report and delete. In Gmail: ⋮ → Report phishing.
On a phone, long-press a link to preview the real destination before tapping.
6 min read

Open Wi-Fi is convenient because it asks for nothing — not even proof that it is the network it claims to be.

  • Treat open Wi-Fi as public. Anyone nearby can be watching.
  • Avoid banking and logins on café, airport or hotel networks.
  • Use mobile data — or a trusted VPN — for sensitive tasks.
  • Turn off auto-join and forget networks you no longer use.
  • Watch for “evil twin” hotspots that copy a real café's name.
  • HTTPS is the minimum, not a guarantee: a fake page can still have a padlock.
If a captive portal asks you to install a profile, certificate or app — walk away.
4 min read

Every permission you grant is data or control you hand over. A flashlight does not need your messages.

  • Install from the official store, not from a forwarded APK.
  • Question every permission. SMS, contacts and accessibility access are high-risk.
  • Review permissions monthly and revoke what an app no longer needs.
  • Remove apps you do not use. Old apps stop getting fixes.
  • Keep “install unknown apps” off in system settings.
  • Update apps and the OS — or enable automatic updates.
A screen-sharing request during a “support call” can let a stranger read your banking app.
5 min read

Scammers rarely break in. They talk someone into opening the door — and the phone is where that happens.

  • A verification code is for you only. Never read it to anyone, ever.
  • “Wrong number” chats that turn flirty or friendly often end in an investment or crypto scam.
  • Voice and video can be faked. Verify money requests on a second channel.
  • Family emergency + new number + urgent money is a classic script.
  • Hang up and call back on a number you already know.
  • Police, bank or “support” calls never need remote access to your phone.
Scammers target people, not systems. Being polite is not worth being robbed.
6 min read

An update closes a door attackers already know about. A backup is what lets you walk away from a ransom demand.

  • Enable automatic updates for the OS, browser and messaging apps.
  • Reboot when asked. A pending update is still an open door.
  • Follow 3-2-1: three copies, on two kinds of media, one off-site.
  • Test a restore at least once. An untested backup is a hope.
  • Keep one backup offline, so ransomware cannot encrypt it too.
Your phone's cloud backup protects photos and chats — but not your whole life. Back up the important bits on purpose.
4 min read

Privacy is not about having nothing to hide. It is about limiting what a stranger can use against you later.

  • Share less. Birth date, location, school and pet names are password-reset material.
  • Use a 6+ digit screen lock and enable biometrics where it helps.
  • Set a SIM PIN to slow down SIM-swap attacks.
  • Turn off ad tracking and review which apps can see your location.
  • Sign up for breach alerts; freeze credit if identity data leaks.
  • Audit privacy settings every few months — defaults change.
Post the holiday photo after you get home, not while your house is empty.
5 min read

Panic causes more damage than the attacker. Work through this list in order.

  • Disconnect from Wi-Fi and mobile data if you suspect malware.
  • From a clean device, change the password of the affected account — and of its recovery email.
  • Revoke active sessions and app passwords, then turn on 2FA.
  • Check recovery email and phone were not changed by someone else.
  • Tell your bank immediately if money or cards may be involved.
  • Report it to your local cybercrime portal (in India: cybercrime.gov.in) and keep screenshots.
Do not pay a ransom and do not trust “recovery” services that message you first.
